The Rising Compass Methodology

The Rising Compass analyzes and tracks the messages found in popular music.

The Rising Compass reads what popular music is saying. Not how it sounds, not if it's catchy, not if it's good or bad. It reads and analyzes the messages contained in the lyrics of songs. It is a clinical tool that emits data. It does not pass judgement or have an opinion. It reflects, with precision, what the lyrics dominating culture are actually saying.

The Rising Compass exists because the founder wanted a way to prove that the lyrical content of popular music was heading in a negative direction. Doing that required a visualization that was easy to understand and based on a controlled data set. It also required a way to gather that data, which meant automation of the thinking and classification of the lyrics themselves.

What follows is the full methodology: where it came from, the calibrator that charges a song, what a charge does and doesn't claim, the gears behind the calibrator, the forces that mold those gears, and the platform that surfaces the result.

Where It Came From

Origin of The Rising Compass

Sound is physical. A song leaving a speaker is pressure moving through air and into the body, where it moves the small hairs of the inner ear and reaches the parts of the brain that handle emotion before any thought catches up. Music doesn't describe a feeling. It installs one. And it does that at a scale most people never stop to picture: every car radio and pair of earbuds, every storefront and stadium speaker, pushing the same handful of songs into millions of nervous systems at once.

That is a real field, not a metaphor for one. The sum of what we are all hearing right now is a kind of collective weather of the mind, and weather affects our lives.

Our founder, Chad Lewine, described seeing this twenty years before there was any way to measure it. The cars were always blasting it into the shared air. There was just no needle. The Rising Compass is the needle.

The Compass operationalizes a specific prior observation. On November 12, 2024, Chad published Popular Music Has Lost Its Moral Compass — a song by song manual reading of every Billboard Year-End Hot 100 top 10 from 1960 to today, calibrated against an explicit three-tier rubric (positive / neutral / negative). 700+ songs, 65 years. The finding was direct: positively charged lyrical content has measurably declined, decade by decade. StatSignificant has documented the same drift through a different methodology, and critics including The Honest Broker have reached the same conclusion from the industry data.

The Rising Compass is that observation turned into infrastructure. The three-tier rubric expanded into the current 58-tenet, five-tier system. The retrospective archive is being recalibrated through the new rubric and folded into the Rising Compass library. What was a published essay now runs as a live instrument — song-by-song, day-by-day, at the resolution culture is actually produced.

The Lens That Charges a Song

The Calibrator

2a

How The Rising Compass analyzes a song

Below is the process by which the Calibrator—the part of The Rising Compass that actually does the analyzing—generates all of the data points that go into the "charge" of a song. The AI agent (currently Claude Opus) runs the song through the calibrator, step by step. The calibrator, not the agent, sets the final number.

Step 1

The lyrics arrive

A song comes in as words on a page. No artist, no chart history, no sound. Just the lyrics.

Step 2

Forget the artist

Who made it and everything around it is set aside.

Step 3

The gut read recorded

Before any analysis, the agent records a first impression and keeps it. Every later step is checked against it.

Step 4

Name the route

The song's path to its tier is named: encouragement, witness, protest, inner work, among others. The route decides which tests apply.

Step 5

Read both axes

Harm and uplift are weighed at once, with a required call: does harm run through the whole song, or sit in one corner?

UpliftHarm

Step 6

Place against precedents

The song is set next to rulings the calibrator already stands behind: stronger than this, gentler than that, somewhere in between.

Step 7

Fine-tune the spot

Four small dials nudge the song's exact position inside its band, without ever crossing into another tier.

The pivot

Findings become a number

The agent reports what it found and never names the charge. The calibrator's math sets the number.

Step 8

Compose the charge

The findings become one charge through fixed arithmetic. The same reading always yields the same number.

Step 9

Derive the tier

The five tiers each cover a fixed range of the scale. Whichever range the charge lands in is the tier.

Step 10

Combine the readings

Read more than once, a song settles on the middle value of its readings, so one odd reading can't drag the verdict.

The result

The verdict

One charge and one tier, on a single scale from all-out war to all-out peace.

2b

The Calibration System

Charge Tiers

Every song is calibrated into one of five tiers. The tiers form a perspective spectrum — from collective consciousness at the top to ego black-hole at the bottom.

Ascended

Collective perspective. The lyrics speak from, to, or about something larger than any one person. Community, humanity, the divine. Universal love, prophetic witness, collective healing. The song's purpose is selfless.

Elevated

Self without ego. Honest internal work on the page. The narrator questions their own behavior, takes accountability, processes through something with visible movement. Growth demonstrated, not just wished for. Vulnerability without performance.

Decent

Catch-all. The lyrics don't push in either direction. Pleasant, fun, romantic, sad, nostalgic — but no internal work being done and no ego being served. The song is what it is. This is the baseline for popular music, and it's not a failure.

Degraded

Ego self. The lyrics serve the narrator's ego, promote avoidance, or steer away from growth. Surface-level status markers as aspiration. Manipulation framed as love. Wallowing without movement. Blame without accountability.

Corrupted

Ego black-hole. The lyrics actively destroy, dehumanize, or consume. Explicit objectification, substance celebration as identity, violence as entertainment, possession as desire. The song's core stance is destruction — of self, of others, of meaning itself.

Charge Value

Every song receives a charge value on one scale from +100 to −100, including 0. The value places the song both within its tier and across the full spectrum. Ascended songs carry high positive charges, Corrupted songs carry deep negative ones, while Decent songs hover around dead-center zero, the baseline.

The five tiers do not divide that scale evenly. Elevated, Decent, and Degraded each span 50 points, while Ascended (+75 to +100) and Corrupted (−75 to −100) span only 25. The poles are narrow on purpose. Reaching them takes more extreme messaging in the lyrics, and the neutral middle, centered on zero, is where most music naturally lands.

Why "Decent" and Not "Good"

The middle tier is deliberately called Decent — not "balanced," not "good," not "healthy." A song that's just entertainment, just filling time, just pleasant background noise — that's fine. It's decent. But The Rising Compass exists to show that music can do more.

2c

Special Conditions

Contamination

Contamination flags songs that carry content undercutting their substance: substance promotion, casual objectification, ego-driven intentions hidden inside otherwise genuine processing. (Contamination applies only to the top 3 tiers — Ascended, Elevated, and Decent.)

A substance reference is not contamination. Substance promotion is. The Rising Compass intentionally distinguishes between a song acknowledging something and promoting or glorifying something.

Contamination can be dangerous because that is how subtle degraded or corrupted messages make their way, kind of like a Trojan horse. The song sounds fine for the most part, but part of the lyrics deliver something else underneath.

The contamination tag does not influence or alter a song's charge, it's only a warning for the listener.

☢

Dogma Reference

Dogma Reference, like Contamination, is a tag, not a value modifier. It doesn't change the song's charge in any way. It marks songs where a specific doctrinal framework (Christian, Islamic, Karmic/Dharmic, or a named institution) is doing the song's work.

The tag fires only when worshipping inside, proclaiming, converting to, or asserting the doctrine is what the song is for. Naming the sacred does not fire it. A passing scriptural allusion used to illustrate a point in an otherwise secular song does not fire it.

The Swap Test. The calibrator runs dogma-suspected songs through a "Swap Test", which replaces the doctrine-specific image with a cross-traditional one. If the song's message survives, the framework was decoration — no tag. If the message collapses because the framework was the engine, the tag fires. The tag exists to make doctrinal content visible, never to judge it.

The Rubric

How Songs Are Read

The calibrator evaluates what the words say, not how the song sounds. Production, melody, and vocal tone are irrelevant. A melancholic track with degraded lyrics is degraded. A catchy track with thoughtful lyrics isn't automatically shallow.

The calibrator reads lyrics sequentially — line by line, accumulating meaning the way a human reads a poem. Each line reshapes what came before it. The same words mean different things depending on what preceded them. After reading the full song, the dominant arc is identified. That is what gets calibrated.

Part 1: The Tenets

It is important to ground your understanding of The Rising Compass calibrator through what are called "the tenets." A tenet is a core principle, belief, or doctrine shared by a group, organization, or philosophy. In this case, the tenets were created based on what is assumed to be humanity's understanding and agreement of what is good, bad and neutral for the individual or society at large.

These 58 tenets are the baseline on which all evaluation is made. The tenets are owned by an external platform, Rising Compass' parent entity, Libra Engine, via the Libra Engine Compass. Tenets are amendable, by the public, via Libra Engine's governance department.

View the tenets

Part 2: The Agent

Claude Opus, Anthropic's most capable model, is the reader. It works through the lyrics against the full rubric and reports what it found, tenet by tenet, without ever naming the charge. Fixed arithmetic turns those findings into the number. Every calibration is reviewed and can be corrected, and calibrated songs become reference points for future calibrations of other songs.

A single reading is never the final word. Every submission or manual recalibration re-runs the lyrics through the calibrator with no memory of that song's own previous runs, and each run is logged with its own tier, charge, summary, and confidence. The canonical calibration drifts toward the confidence-weighted mean as runs accumulate. Where two runs disagree, the Calibration History shows both rather than averaging the disagreement out of sight.

Why Both Together

The agent brings the same patience and the same rubric to every song. No favoritism, no fatigue across thousands of songs. The tenets ensure it isn't reading from its own taste: what counts as Ascended, Elevated, Degraded, or Corrupted is a published, contestable artifact, not a private opinion buried in a model.

Reading without published criteria is critique. Criteria without reading is rhetoric. A calibration is a reading against criteria, both visible and both accountable.

Start at Zero

Every song starts at Decent — charge zero. This is not a judgment. It's the starting position. From zero, the calibrator must build a case using specific lyrical evidence applied against the tenets to move the needle in either direction. If no clear case can be built, the song stays Decent. The burden of proof increases with distance from zero.

Topics Don't Determine Tiers

The calibrator reads messaging — what the lyrics say and do on the page. Not topics. The same topic can land at any tier depending on what the lyrics contain. Love, struggle, partying, faith, heartbreak — all neutral. What the song does with the topic is what is measured.

Misreads, Amendments, Deliberations

Public Forces At Play

Every constitution that survives has an amendment process. Ours is public and accountable by design. The forces below are not failure modes. They are the mechanism. Disagreement is data. Every flagged misread is a breakdown in our collective reasoning made tangible, surfaced so we can debate it in public and resolve it in a way both sides can live with.

Force One

Auxiliary Measurements Per Song

Each song detail page shows additional independent signals that expand a song's data beyond the main charge suite:

  • The calibrator's reading — the song's canonical tier and charge, settled across every run the song has had. The mechanism is in The Rubric.
  • Audience Vibe — a separate democratic needle anyone can push once per year. The crowd gets to place the song where they think it belongs, independent of the calibrator's reading.
  • The gap between them — when the calibrator and the crowd diverge, that divergence is a signal. Either the rubric has a blind spot the public is exposing, or the crowd is drifting and the calibrator is holding the line. Both outcomes are valuable data.
Force Two

Misread & Satirical Reports

Every song page has a link to file a report if you disagree with a calibration. Two kinds of reports:

  • Misread — the calibrator read the lyrics wrong, missed context, or applied a tenet incorrectly.
  • Satirical — the song depicts behavior to expose it rather than endorse it. The calibrator reads literally on first pass (words are words), and satire requires human verification. When a satire flag is verified, an admin can invoke a recalibration under a parallel satire tenet set.

Reports trigger manual review. Nothing auto-recalibrates. The reasoning for every accepted flag is public.

File a misread report

Force Three

The Public Calibration Log

When a calibration changes, the reasoning is shown permanently on the song's page and aggregated in the public log:

  • Recalibrations — when a song's calibration is changed (via accepted satire flag, public-interest review, or consensus drift across runs), the before/after charge and an admin-written public summary are logged.
  • Resets — when an entry is returned to an uncalibrated state (duplicates, corrections, incomplete lyrics), the reason is recorded publicly. The row persists so every downstream reference still resolves.
  • Runs — every calibration run ever fired on a song, chronologically, with its full summary.

Open the calibration log

Force Four

Amendments to the Tenets

The tenets themselves are amendable. Amendments are public from the moment they're proposed. The discourse happens off-site, in the open. The Libra Engine Compass keeps the record. Every proposal is visible, with its full reasoning and current status.

This is the slowest force, by design. A constitution that can be amended in a week is a flag in the wind. A constitution that can never be amended is an authoritarian fossil. The amendment process is deliberately slow and deliberately public. High burden of change. Full transparency.

See the amendment record

Mistakes are part of the process. Every flagged misread, every recalibration, every amendment is a made-tangible breakdown in our collective reasoning. The breakdown gets surfaced and debated, with a public record of how it was resolved. Where we agree becomes ratified. Where we disagree becomes discourse. More peaceful, more conscious communities come from finding both.

The calibrator gets rebuilt in the open. In June 2026 the calibrator's procedure was rebuilt from the ground up, with the tenets kept word for word. Replacing "Explicit"

The New Label

The old explicit label is dead

The music industry's explicit content label is a binary, age-gated warning: "this has bad words, keep it from kids." It was designed for a world where access could be restricted. That world is gone. Every song ever recorded is one tap away from anyone of any age. The age restriction is functionally dead.

What remains is a black-and-white sticker that tells you nothing about what the music is actually doing to you. A song that glorifies materialism, ego worship, and objectification without saying a single "explicit" word gets no label at all. Meanwhile a song that processes grief with one f-bomb gets flagged.

The Replacement

The Rising Compass Badge

The Rising Compass badge tells you the charge of the song's main message—what programming is contained in the lyrics. It's not about censoring words. It's not age-restricted. It's just there. A living, color-coded diagnostic hydrated in real time from The Rising Compass:

  • The tier (Ascended → Corrupted) — the color band
  • The charge value (−100 to +100) — the precise number
  • The charge summary — one-line plain-language description of what's in the lyrics
  • The contamination flag — when higher-tier substance is undermined by lower-tier content

The badge is not a static image. It's an embedded, API-hydrated element — the same technology lawyer and trust-verification sites use. An artist or platform registers for an account on Rising Compass, receives an embed code tied to their account, and the badge renders live from Rising Compass data. Rising Compass controls the calibration. The artist controls where it appears.

The Intent

Move the compass by creating

The point is not to ask anyone to stop making anything. The point is to make what music is doing visible. Artists who want to elevate the frequency can do it on purpose. Listeners who want to choose differently can do as they please.

If you want the compass to point a different direction, the path is to make art that resonates with what you want to see in the world. The compass moves when art moves. That is the entire mechanism. Restriction is not the lever. Creation is.

Closing

It moves with us

The Rising Compass is not tied to a moment in time. Not to a place. Not to a particular generation's taste. It is tied to us: the people interacting with each other here on earth, in the communities and nations we share. The tenets are what we agree to while we share this culture. They will move when we do.

Topics vs. Messaging

Common Questions & Challenges

The same topic, four different tiers

The most common source of confusion. People assume certain topics are inherently good or bad. They're not. Here's how the same topic lands at different tiers:

Love is a topic. It can carry any frequency. A love song about growing together and becoming better people is Elevated. A love song about "I love you, you love me, we're happy" is Decent — pleasant, not harmful, but just treading water. A love song about getting drunk together is Degraded. A love song about possession, objectification, or reducing a person to a body is Corrupted. Same topic. Four different tiers.

A breakup song that processes grief honestly — sits in the pain, learns something, moves through it with dignity — is Elevated. A breakup song that agitates contempt, revenge fantasies, or wallows in self-pity is Degraded or Corrupted. Processing vs. agitating. That's the whole distinction.

They're not — automatically. A party song that's pure fun with no harmful payload is Decent. But most chart party songs carry substance celebration, objectification, or ego worship as the actual content. The "party" is the cover.

Normal, yes. Automatically elevated, no. The cultural assumption that pairing off, dating, and romantic attraction are inherently positive is part of what the calibrator examines. A relationship song that demonstrates growth, mutual elevation, or genuine vulnerability earns its way up. One that just describes being in love — surface-level coupling as content — is Decent at best. The bar isn't "is this about love?" The bar is "what does this love do?"

Struggle and pain are topics, not virtues. A song about struggle that processes it with dignity and helps the listener grow is Elevated. A song that glamorizes suffering, wallows without direction, or uses pain as ego currency is Degraded. Naming real pain isn't enough. What the song does with that pain is what matters.

It is art. Nobody said it wasn't. But art isn't automatically good for you. Fast food is real food. It's engineered, it's everywhere, and people love it. It's also destructive, and it still wrecks your body whether you enjoy eating it or not. Some music works the same way. Real creative work that still carries a destructive message. The calibrator doesn't measure whether something is art. It measures what the lyrics actually say.
